Working Papers

Occupational Mobility and the Price of Persistence with Joanna B. Upton Draft, August 2026. To be presented at NEUDC 2026. Draft available upon request.
Abstract

How readily do poor rural households move between occupations, and what holds them in place? Using a 51-month panel of 4,469 households in southern Malawi, observed each agricultural season, we estimate a dynamic model of households’ occupational portfolios in which a credit constraint limits who can finance capital-intensive activities and a labor-market friction prices access and switching. Because persistent heterogeneity can masquerade as friction, each friction parameter is estimated with and without household-history controls and reported as a bracket. Three results follow. Switching is costly: revising the portfolio costs 21 to 48 percent of the dispersion in seasonal taste shocks per activity changed, and this friction alone accounts for 23 to 56 percent of persistence beyond what memoryless choice predicts. Incumbency matters: past operators of an occupation face lower entry barriers than first-time entrants, most strongly for salaried work. And liquidity determines who participates, not when: households without a liquid buffer operate capital-intensive activities at lower rates in every season, whatever the state of the credit market, making credit protection the one policy lever that reaches them.

Shifting Winds: Shocks and the Self-Selection of Migrants in Malawi with Dana J. Smith and Joanna B. Upton Draft in progress.
Abstract

Whether migrants come from the top or the bottom of the skill distribution determines who gains from migration, but data that observe migrants before they leave are rare in sub-Saharan Africa. We study migrant self-selection in Malawi using the Rapid Feedback Monitoring System, a monthly panel that followed 5,400 households in 12 districts from August 2020 to January 2025, recording departures in real time alongside self-reported shocks. Nearly one in five households sent a migrant. Moves are long-term rather than seasonal, and most migrants stay within their district or go to Malawian cities. Selection is positive but modest: English literacy predicts migration while general schooling does not. Within households, selection is sharper: families send their most-educated member, and a man when one is available. Across households, family structure predicts migration and wealth does not. Drought, rising food prices, illness, and economic losses each raise migration modestly for up to a year, and they reinforce rather than reverse selection: larger households become still more likely to send a migrant, and women still less likely to go.

Publications

Stable Portfolios, Costly Churn: A High-Frequency View of Diversification and Transition in Rural Malawi Food Policy, 2026.
Abstract

Rural Malawian households adjust labor along two margins of a single portfolio: churning occupations over time (transition) and holding several income activities at once (concurrent diversification). Annual surveys collapse the year into a single observation and record sequential within-year reallocation as though it were a stable diversified portfolio. Using a monthly panel of more than 5,400 households (2020–2024), I separate the two and trace their distinct and opposing food-security signatures. A household’s diversification is assembled mostly from different members each holding a distinct, short-duration activity, rather than from one person juggling several activities at once (individual multitasking), and primary occupations churn horizontally through low-return casual labor (ganyu). Decomposing the within-year food-security association into two offsetting flows reveals that transitions track deterioration, disproportionately for the asset-poor, while stable diversification is protective; I interpret these associations descriptively. An annual instrument of the kind used by the World Bank’s Living Standards Measurement Study (LSMS), built from the same households, recovers the consumption–diversification gradient but cannot separate the two flows, even though they call for opposite responses: distress-driven switching marks households that need to be shielded from being pushed into low-return work, whereas stable diversification is worth protecting and encouraging.
